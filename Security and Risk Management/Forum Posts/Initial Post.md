The integration of data-driven technologies into human rights investigations offers transformative advantages in documentation and scale; however, as Hancock et al. (2024) observe, these tools often aggravate risks to fairness and accountability. From a risk science perspective, the "black box" nature of algorithmic analysis introduces significant "uncertainty" regarding the reliability of investigative outcomes (Aven and Thekdi, 2025). When investigators rely on automated systems to categorise evidence, a lack of algorithmic transparency can obscure inherent biases within training datasets. If these datasets reflect historical systemic inequalities, the technology may inadvertently reproduce those biases, undermining the pursuit of justice.

A pertinent case study is the implementation of iris-scanning technology for aid distribution in refugee camps, such as the UNHCR’s Azraq camp in Jordan. While tools like the "EyePay" system are designed to enhance efficiency and reduce fraud, they often operate in a legal vacuum where national data protection regulations are weak or non-existent (Noone, 2022). Critics argue that this creates a power imbalance where "informed consent" is effectively coerced, as refugees may feel they must submit to biometric enrolment to receive life-sustaining aid. This mirrors Hubbard’s (2020) argument that risk management failures frequently stem from a lack of proper input from those most affected. In such contexts, "design from a distance" fails to account for the risk that centralised biometric databases could be seized or misused, transforming a protective tool into a high-stakes surveillance liability.

To mitigate these risks, organisations must move beyond traditional IT security and adopt a Human Rights by Design (HRbD) framework. Adhering to the NIST (2023) Risk Management Framework, developers should conduct Human Rights Impact Assessments (HRIAs) and integrate multi-stakeholder oversight into the Software Development Lifecycle (SDLC). By embedding accountability mechanisms and audit trails, as recommended by Stoneburner, Goguen, and Feringa (2002), we can ensure technology serves as a transparent instrument of justice rather than an opaque amplifier of risk.
