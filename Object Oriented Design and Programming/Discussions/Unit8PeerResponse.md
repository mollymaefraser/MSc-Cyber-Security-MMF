I think your closing point is the strongest part of this post, that the aim of refactoring is not the most sophisticated design but the right amount of complexity for what the requirements actually justify. That is a useful corrective, since it is easy to treat "apply the named pattern" as the goal in itself rather than a means to an end, especially in an exercise like this one where the Strategy Pattern is explicitly being taught.

Your staged approach (constants first, Strategy only if the discount rules actually grow) matches what I did in my own post too, though I framed it as two separate options rather than a sequence. Reading yours makes me think the sequencing framing is actually better, since it more honestly reflects how refactoring tends to happen in practice, incrementally and in response to real pressure, rather than jumping straight to the most general solution up front.

One thing I would push on: you say two discount types is still "understandable" as an if-elif chain, and I agree, but where do you think that threshold actually sits? Three types? Five? I ask because "readable enough" is doing a lot of work in your argument and in mine, and it would be useful to pin down what would actually trigger the move to Strategy for you, beyond just "if it grows." Is it a fixed number of branches, or more about whether the discount logic itself becomes non-trivial, for example if a discount depended on quantity or user tier rather than just item type?

Also, the Clausen citation on refactoring in small steps to reduce the risk of changing behaviour is a good one to bring in. Did Clausen say anything about how you would actually verify that behaviour has not changed at each step, for example through characterisation tests, or is that left to the reader to figure out?
